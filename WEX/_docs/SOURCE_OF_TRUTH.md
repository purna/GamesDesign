The lesson is about choosing and requesting a second, specialism-focused work placement, meeting the deadline, and behaving professionally while on placement. Here are some game ideas built around those goals.

1. Placement Planning Challenge
Teams get a fictional student profile, such as a film student hoping to work on a shoot. They choose a placement option, then decide what information the student needs before requesting it on Navigate.

Give points for a suitable choice, complete request details, and noticing practical constraints such as college hours or agreed work times. This practises matching a placement to a specialism and preparing a realistic request.

2. Deadline Dash
Teams get event cards such as “Skills Week starts 8 March” and “The employer needs a safety check.” They arrange the events in order and work out when to submit the request. Add a few “curveballs”—for example, a placement that needs a DBS check—to prompt discussion about why the deadline matters.

3. What Would You Do?
Students draw a placement scenario and choose the best response. Examples: “You’re running late,” “Your timesheet is incomplete,” or “Someone asks you to film a colleague.” Award points for safe, professional choices, including who to contact when a problem comes up.

4. Placement Options Pitch
Each team gets one option—such as a block placement, employer visit, volunteering, or live project brief—and prepares a short pitch explaining which student or specialism it might suit. The class votes on which option best fits a sample student’s goals.

5. Find the Missing Details
Show a mock placement request with several details missing: employer contact, dates, agreed hours, tutor name, or tasks. Teams race to spot what’s missing and explain why each detail matters.

Best fit for this one-hour lesson: use Placement Planning Challenge as the main activity, then finish with a quick What Would You Do? round to reinforce professionalism, consent, logging hours, and getting help. The slides also mention a Charleston creative skills placement, which could work as a specific example in the challenge.

Web game plan: Race to the Role
A competitive career-building game where students choose a target job, build relevant experience, and race against AI candidates. The point is to show that getting a job takes more than collecting activities: students need to choose opportunities that fit their goal, complete the work, and turn it into credible CV evidence.

The game loop
Each round represents part of a student’s journey from college towards employment.

Choose a target job. The player picks a role they want to win, such as esports event assistant, junior games artist, film production runner, or digital marketing assistant.
Plan the next move. They choose how to spend their available time: arrange an opportunity, complete an assignment, attend an experience, improve a skill, or recover.
Navigate the opportunity. For placements and other experiences, players work through the steps needed to set them up. Incomplete or unsuitable plans can delay or block the opportunity.
Complete the work. Choices and skill checks determine what the student does and whether they can gather strong evidence.
Update the CV. Finished work adds a CV item, skill, reference, or portfolio piece.
Compete for the job. When players meet the role’s requirements, they apply. The winner is the eligible candidate with the strongest role fit and evidence.
The game can run as a classroom race, with students playing individually or in teams. AI candidates take their own turns and make visible progress towards the same job.

Opportunity routes
All routes use a shared sequence: choose a relevant opportunity → arrange it properly → take part → reflect on what was learned → add evidence to the CV. Each route has different setup actions.

Opportunity	What the player needs to do in the game	Example CV evidence
Work placement	Choose a role linked to the target job; find an employer; agree tasks, dates, days and hours; collect the employer’s contact details and address; check the hours and any approval requirements; submit a complete request through Navigate; attend and log hours.	Tasks completed, hours, skills demonstrated, employer reference
Employer visit	Find a relevant visit; check the date, location and joining arrangements; book or confirm a place; prepare questions; attend and follow up with a reflection or contact.	Industry knowledge, professional questions, contact made
Volunteering	Find an activity with a genuine organisation need; check responsibilities, dates, location and supervision; agree the commitment; attend and record contributions.	Responsibility, teamwork, community contribution, referee
Live project brief	Review the brief and deadline; clarify the client’s needs; plan the work; complete and present the output; respond to feedback; save the final work for a portfolio.	Project outcome, problem-solving, presentation, portfolio item
Industry partnership	Find a course or college-linked opportunity; apply or confirm participation; understand the partner’s expectations; attend sessions or complete agreed work; build a professional connection and capture outcomes.	Industry collaboration, specialist skills, project or event evidence
The game should distinguish opportunities students can arrange themselves from those that depend on college or employer availability. For instance, an employer visit may require a booking, while a live brief or industry partnership may be offered through a course.

Make the pathways connect to the job
Each target job has a role profile with a few key requirements, such as technical skill, communication, reliability, teamwork, and portfolio evidence. Activities contribute different evidence:

A placement can build practical experience and a reference.
An employer visit can build industry understanding and useful contacts.
Volunteering can show reliability, initiative, and teamwork.
A live brief can create a piece of work to show in a portfolio.
An industry partnership can provide specialist feedback and experience working with professionals.
A “Why this helps” panel explains the connection after each action. For example:

You completed a live project brief. You now have a portfolio piece that shows you can respond to a client’s needs. This supports the “relevant work” requirement for Junior Games Artist.

This keeps the learning visible without making every choice a lecture.

CV and job readiness
The CV is a live part of the game. Each entry shows:

the experience and organisation
the player’s role and tasks
a skill demonstrated
an outcome or example of work
a reference or contact, if earned
how strongly the evidence matches the target job
Players can earn an experience, but they only get a strong CV entry by completing it and recording what they did. A visit that was booked but missed does not become a completed CV item. A finished project with no explanation earns less credit than one with a clear outcome and reflection.

AI competitors
Each AI candidate has a target-job strategy and visible strengths:

The Planner submits requests early and avoids deadline trouble.
The Portfolio Builder prioritises live briefs and finished work.
The Networker focuses on visits and industry partnerships.
The All-Rounder balances experience, assignments, and wellbeing.
AI candidates should make their choices visible and explain them briefly, so they model different strategies rather than acting as mystery opponents. They can make mistakes too: miss a deadline, choose an irrelevant opportunity, or neglect an assignment.

Days off, failed assignments, and grades
Setbacks should affect readiness, time, and evidence without implying that one bad result permanently ends a student’s prospects.

Taking a day off: If the player misses a scheduled experience without arranging it, they may lose the day’s learning, damage reliability evidence, or need to reschedule. If they are unwell and contact the employer and college, they can protect their professional conduct score.
Missing an assignment: The player loses time or a skill checkpoint needed for an opportunity. They can use a later turn to catch up, but it may delay their application.
Failing an assignment: The player may not earn the associated skill evidence yet. They can seek feedback, practise, and resubmit in a later round.
Getting a distinction: Strong grades can add evidence of subject knowledge and may help meet a job’s criteria. They should boost a candidate’s readiness, not automatically beat practical experience or a relevant portfolio.
Not getting a distinction: The player can still win by building relevant experience, completing strong work, and showing their skills. Grades matter only where the role profile says they matter.
A simple wellbeing meter can discourage players from choosing every possible activity. Overloading themselves increases the chance of missed work or low-quality evidence. Rest and planning can help them stay consistent.

Race and winning
Players do not win just by collecting the most activities. To qualify for the prize job, they must meet the role’s minimum requirements, such as:

at least one relevant experience
a required skill or assignment
one piece of job-related evidence
a completed application step
Among eligible candidates, the prize goes to the first player who completes the application with enough relevant evidence. If no one qualifies by the end of the game, the winner is the player closest to job-ready, and the class discusses what each candidate still needs.

This rewards progress and planning, while showing that a missed day or lower grade can slow someone down without making success impossible.

Example round: esports event assistant
Target job requirements: teamwork, event organisation, reliability, and experience with esports or live events.

The player could:

Visit an employer event to learn how tournaments are run.
Volunteer at a local tournament and record their responsibilities.
Complete a live project brief creating a match schedule or promotional graphics.
Request a placement with an esports organisation, confirming the role, contact details, dates, tasks, and suitable hours.
Add completed outcomes and reflections to their CV.
Apply once the role’s minimum evidence requirements are met.
The game can show how these experiences reinforce one another: the visit informs the project, the project helps the player contribute during volunteering, and the placement gives them practical evidence and a possible reference.

Race to the Role: Gaming Industry Edition
A turn-based classroom game for esports students building experience and evidence towards any job in the gaming industry: esports events, game development, streaming, QA testing, community management, marketing, production, art, and more.

Players: 2–5, individually or in teams
Play time: 25–40 minutes
Goal: Be the first player to meet the requirements for a gaming-industry job and submit a complete application.

What you need
One Target Job card per player
Opportunity, Assignment, and Event cards
One CV tracker per player
A token or marker for each player
Three AI competitor cards, controlled by the teacher or by simple rules
A player’s CV tracker records: experience, skills, portfolio evidence, reliability, and application readiness.

Set up
Each player draws or chooses a Target Job card.
Deal each player two Opportunity cards and one Assignment card.
Place the remaining cards in separate face-down decks.
Set each player’s Energy to 3. Energy represents available time and focus; it is not a measure of personal worth.
Introduce the AI competitors. They pursue their own gaming-industry jobs and use the same turn rules as players.
Taking a turn
On your turn, take two actions. You may take the same action twice.

Explore: Draw two Opportunity cards, keep one, and discard the other.
Set up: Play an Opportunity card and complete its setup checklist. If information is missing, spend an action to contact the employer or organiser and fill it in.
Take part: Complete a ready Opportunity or Assignment card. Follow its outcome and add any earned evidence to your CV.
Study or practise: Spend one Energy to gain a Skill token that fits your target job, or work on an Assignment card.
Recover: Restore one Energy, up to the starting maximum of 3.
Apply: If you meet your Target Job’s requirements, submit your application.
At the end of your turn, draw back up to three cards in your hand. Resolve an Event card when instructed by the teacher, or at the start of each new round.

Setting up opportunities
Before an opportunity is ready, the player must complete its setup requirements. These vary by card, but may include:

identifying the organisation and opportunity
confirming the employer or organiser contact
agreeing the role, tasks, dates, and times
checking location, travel, equipment, and any rules
submitting a request or booking
checking the college’s approval process for placements
A placement request must have complete information before the player can take part. The lesson’s placement rules can be used in the game: agreed tasks, dates, days and times; employer name and full address; employer contact name, phone and email; and tutor name. Placements must fit college hours (8:30am–5:30pm), with a maximum eight-hour day. Students should agree their hours in writing before requesting. Teachers should update the game’s dates and requirements for their cohort.

Important: An opportunity becomes CV evidence only after the player completes it and records what they did. Drawing or setting up an opportunity alone does not add it to the CV.

Assignments, days off, and setbacks
The game treats setbacks as things to respond to and recover from.

Missed assignment: Lose one action next turn to catch up, or take a Feedback token and complete a recovery task.
Assignment not passed: Don’t gain its Skill or Portfolio evidence yet. Spend an action reviewing feedback and try again on a later turn.
Day off: If an Event card says you need a day off, lose one action. If you also spend an action to contact the employer or tutor, you keep your Reliability token.
Low Energy: If you have no Energy, you can still take actions, but you cannot take on a new Assignment until you recover.
Distinction: Some assignments offer a distinction outcome. Earning it gives a bonus skill or evidence point, but a distinction is never required unless the Target Job card says so.
A player can always recover, seek feedback, or choose a different opportunity. One setback does not remove them from the game.

Winning the job
Each Target Job card lists minimum requirements. When you meet them, use an action to Apply.

The first eligible player to apply wins the race. If more than one player applies during the same round, the player with the most relevant evidence wins. If tied, the player with the strongest portfolio evidence wins; if still tied, the players share the win.

To keep the game focused on learning, the teacher can also award a Best Career Plan to the player who explains how their experience connects to their chosen job—even if they did not win the race.

Sample cards
Target Job: Esports Event Assistant
You want to help run tournaments, leagues, and live gaming events.

To apply, collect:

1 completed gaming-industry experience
1 teamwork or organisation Skill
1 event-related CV evidence item
1 Reliability token
Good opportunities: volunteering at a tournament, employer visit to an event organiser, live event brief, placement with an esports organisation.

Target Job: Junior QA Tester
You want to help find bugs and improve how games work.

To apply, collect:

1 completed gaming-related experience
1 testing or problem-solving Skill
1 piece of evidence showing a bug report, test plan, or quality check
Good opportunities: QA placement, testing brief, studio visit, game jam or development project.

Target Job: Gaming Community Assistant
You want to help gaming communities stay active, welcoming, and safe.

To apply, collect:

1 completed experience involving communication or audiences
1 Communication Skill
1 evidence item showing a post, event, moderation plan, or community activity
Good opportunities: volunteering at an event, community project brief, employer visit, industry partnership.

Opportunity: Employer Visit — Game Studio
Setup: Confirm the visit date, location, booking, and any travel or access arrangements. Prepare two questions about jobs in the studio.

Take part: Spend one action to attend. Ask a question and record one thing you learned.

CV reward: Add Industry Insight. If your target job matches something you learned, also gain one relevant Skill token.

Reflection prompt: What job or skill did you discover that you hadn’t considered?

Opportunity: Volunteering — Local Esports Tournament
Setup: Confirm the organiser, event date, location, responsibilities, start and finish times, and who will supervise you.

Take part: Choose one role: registration, player support, setup, social posts, or match scheduling.

CV reward: Add Tournament Volunteer and one Skill token: Teamwork, Organisation, Communication, or Technical Setup.

Reliability check: If you have no Reliability token, explain how you will confirm your shift and contact the organiser if plans change. A good answer earns one.

Reflection prompt: What did you do that helped the event run well?

Opportunity: Live Project Brief — Design a Tournament Promo
Setup: Read the brief, identify the audience, check the deadline, and decide what you will make: a poster, short video, social post, or stream overlay.

Take part: Spend one action and 1 Energy to complete and present your work.

CV reward: Add Tournament Promo Project to your portfolio and gain one Skill token: Design, Communication, Planning, or Digital Production.

Bonus: If you also have Industry Insight, gain one extra evidence point because you used what you learned from an employer visit.

Reflection prompt: What feedback would help you improve the work?

Opportunity: Work Placement — Esports Organisation
Setup checklist: Complete every item before you can start.

Organisation name and full address
Employer contact name, phone, and email
Agreed tasks or role
Start and end dates
Agreed days and times
Tutor’s name
Hours fit college rules and have been agreed in writing
Placement request submitted through the required college process
Take part: Choose a focus: event operations, streaming support, social media, player support, or equipment setup. Complete the placement and log what you did.

CV reward: Add Gaming Industry Placement, one relevant Skill token, and a Reference token if the employer provides feedback.

If something is missing: You must spend an action to contact the employer or tutor and complete the request. The placement cannot begin in the game until its setup is approved.

Opportunity: Industry Partnership — Guest Mentor Project
Setup: Find out who the industry partner is, what the project involves, when sessions happen, and what the partner expects you to produce.

Take part: Complete the agreed task and ask for feedback from the partner.

CV reward: Add Industry Collaboration and one Skill token related to your contribution.

Bonus: If you revise your work using the feedback, add one Portfolio Evidence point.

Reflection prompt: What changed in your work after professional feedback?

Assignment: Game Industry Skills Task
Task: Create a short piece of work connected to your target job. Examples include a bug report, match schedule, stream overlay, event plan, social post, or player feedback form.

Choose one:

Complete: Spend one action. Add one relevant Skill token.
Strong work: Spend one action and 1 Energy. Add one Skill token and one Portfolio Evidence point.
Needs improvement: Draw an Event card or take a Feedback token. Spend a later action improving the work to earn the Skill token.
Optional distinction: If you have two relevant Skill tokens, earn a Distinction mark and one extra Portfolio Evidence point. A distinction is a bonus, not a requirement.

Event: Assignment Deadline
You have an assignment due soon.

Choose one:

Spend your next action completing it.
Take a Feedback token and ask your teacher or teammate for help; complete it on a later turn.
Ignore it: lose one Energy and delay your job application by one action.
Event: You’re Unwell on an Experience Day
Take a day off.

If you spend an action contacting both the organiser or employer and your tutor, keep your Reliability token and reschedule the activity. If you don’t contact them, lose one action next turn to arrange a new plan.

Event: New Game Jam Announced
A short game jam is open to students.

If you have 1 Energy, you may spend an action to join. Choose one contribution—design, testing, art, audio, coding, production, or promotion. Completing the jam earns a Game Jam Project CV item and one matching Skill token.

Event: Your First Choice Falls Through
The placement or visit is no longer available.

Keep any skills or evidence you have already earned. Discard that Opportunity card and draw two new ones. You may also ask your tutor or Careers & Employability team for another route.

Optional CV evidence rule
At the end of each completed experience, the player must say or write:

What they did.
Which skill they used.
What they learned or produced.
How it relates to their target job.
If they can explain all four, the CV item is Strong Evidence. If they only name the activity, it is Basic Evidence.

This helps students see that experience becomes more useful on a CV when they can describe their contribution and connect it to the job they want.

### Architecture

Single-page app, no build step. Must be served over HTTP (service worker and `fetch`
of JSON both require it) — `python3 -m http.server` from the project root.

| File | Role |
| --- | --- |
| `index.html` | All markup: header, industry select, setup, Hall of Fame, learn screen, game board, modals |
| `styles.css` | Layout, screens, panels, modals |
| `card-styles.css` | Card faces, drop zones, rival modal, game-over overlay |
| `js/game.js` | Data loading, state, all rendering, player actions, banking, events, persistence |
| `js/ai.js` | Rival decision logic only (`runRivalTurn`, market heuristics, rival turn popup) |
| `js/settings.js` | Theme, sound, animations, auto-draw, reset |
| `js/accessibility.js` | High contrast, large text, TTS, simple mode |
| `sw.js` | Offline cache. Network-first for code and data, cache-first for images |

Scripts are plain deferred `<script>` tags sharing one global scope. There is no
module system, so functions cross-reference freely between `game.js` and `ai.js`.

Analysis, Coaching, Communication, Design, Documentation, Industry Knowledge,
Organisation, Presentation, Problem solving, Reflection, Reliability, Teamwork,
Technical Setup.

### Gotcha: `game-data.json` is the runtime source of truth

**Editing a file in `cards/` or `roles/` changes nothing until `game-data.json` is
regenerated** from those folders. This has caused repeated confusion (an art fix, a
skills change, and a coverage pass all appeared to "not work" because the
consolidated file still held the old data). There is no build script — regeneration
has been done ad hoc. **Adding one is the highest-value next task in this project.**

### Gotcha: the service worker caches aggressively

`sw.js` is network-first for HTML, JS, CSS and game data, and cache-first for images,
so online edits should always land. The previous version was cache-first for
everything, which served stale `game.js` indefinitely and caused the same confusion
repeatedly. If stale content ever appears again, unregister the worker in DevTools →
Application → Service Workers, then hard-reload.

### Gotcha: saved games hold card copies

Cards in `localStorage` are full objects, not id references, so a save freezes the
card data as it was. `resyncCard()` (called from `cleanPile()` on resume) re-reads
each card from current data by id and keeps its `uid`, which repairs art, skill and
route changes inside an in-progress game. The same function drops malformed cards.

### Mechanics as built

- 2 actions per turn; 2 market picks per round (1 action each); 2 returns per round
  (free); 2 market swaps per round; hand cap 6
- Bank a set when the selected cards exactly match a route's required categories.
  The bank button is gated on a real `findBank()` check, not just the card count
- A banked set grants **every** skill on its cards (capped at 2 skills per card)
- Cards granting a skill that some role requires are dealt back into the deck when
  consumed, so required skills can never be exhausted
- Market rotates 2 cards each round; deck reshuffles from discard, then rebuilds
- Events every 3rd round
- Win: meet experience, evidence, set-variety and required-skill gates, then Apply.
  Rivals play visibly turn by turn and can win

### UX affordances added

- Required-skill cards get a gold outline and a `needed` pill in hand and market
- Card face shows one skill plus `+N`; preview shows the full list
- Per-card `ⓘ` button opens the preview on touch (click alone selects)
- Hand can be reordered by dragging; the order is cosmetic (nothing reads hand order)
- "Skills for the role" panel with a `?` tooltip listing routes, required card types
  and a concrete example card per route
- Helper-hints toggle in the header hides the legend, drag hints, guide banner and
  the empty-selection tutorial blurb, but never the live set-builder feedback
- Hall of Fame page and per-run completion history, stored in `localStorage`
- How to Play is a modal overlay, not a panel swap
- Home button returns to the main menu, with a single `showOnlyPanel()` used by every
  navigation path

### Dead files, safe to delete

- `js/app.js` — not loaded by `index.html`; it was the only service worker
  registration, now moved into `game.js`
- Root scripts: `add-cards.js`, `add-more-industry-cards.js`, `build-cards.js`,
  `create-industry-cards.js`, `generate-industry-data.js`, `regenerate-data.js`,
  `script.js` — none referenced by the page
- `data/industries/*/cards.json` and `data/industries/*/cards/cards.json` — generated
  aggregate arrays sitting inside the card folders; they are never loaded, but they
  pollute the folder and break any tooling that globs it
- `prototype/`, `cloud-functions/`, `functions/`, `plugins/` — not part of the app

### Testing

No test framework. Verification during development was done with a throwaway Node
harness (`/tmp/smoke.js`) that stubs the DOM, evals `game.js`, and calls the real
render functions against live game data. It caught bugs that `node --check` cannot
(scope errors, missing DOM lookups). It is worth moving into the repo.

**Always run `node --check` on all JS after edits** — it catches syntax only, and has
missed real runtime errors.

### Not browser-verified

Most UI work has been validated by the harness and by static checks, but the visual
result has not been seen in a browser. Worth a pass over: card face density with
multi-skill labels, the gold key-card outline, tooltip positioning in the sidebar,
the learn-screen overlay at short viewport heights, the hand reorder drop indicator
on a wrapped hand, and the header icon row on narrow screens.
